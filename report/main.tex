\title{\textbf{Observing LLM API Latency During Peak and Off-Peak Hours:\\
A 28-Day Log}}

\author[1]{Jaekyung Noh}
\affil[1]{Independent Researcher \\ \texttt{silverbreak1@gmail.com}}

\date{May 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Commercial large language model (LLM) APIs are shared infrastructure, yet little is known
about how server load during peak business hours affects latency, throughput, and answer
quality in practice.
We benchmark four providers---OpenAI (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}), Anthropic
(\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}), Google (\texttt{gemini-flash-lite}), and Alibaba Qwen
(\texttt{qwen3.5-flash})---twice daily for 28 days using 30 MMLU-Pro questions, measuring
time-to-first-token (TTFT), tokens-per-second (TPS), and answer accuracy.
Peak sessions run at 10:00\,ET (within the 8\,AM--2\,PM ET peak band \citep{anthropic2026promotion});
non-peak sessions run at 22:00\,ET.
Analyzing at the level of complete measurement slots---the appropriate unit of
statistical independence---we find that peak-hour TTFT is directionally higher for the
three US-hosted providers (+3.5\% to +18.1\% in slot medians), though this effect is
only marginally significant and, for Google, not robust to a magnitude-based test.
The most robust result is a reversed pattern for Qwen---served from an Alibaba Cloud
US endpoint---which is \emph{faster} during US peak hours ($-9.4\%$ TTFT, $+13.4\%$ TPS;
significant under both rank- and magnitude-based tests), consistent with the hypothesis
that US peak corresponds to Asia off-peak.
Answer accuracy shows no significant change for any provider across conditions,
suggesting that output quality is decoupled from infrastructure load at the scale tested.
We emphasize that with only nine complete slots the study is underpowered; we therefore
report effect sizes as a directional pilot signal rather than confirmatory findings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language model APIs have become commodity infrastructure for production applications,\footnote{The English writing in this paper was assisted by Claude (Anthropic).}
yet their service-level properties under varying load conditions remain largely undocumented
from the consumer's perspective.
Traffic on commercial AI APIs follows predictable diurnal patterns driven by North American
business hours, and the substantial per-request compute of LLM inference may amplify the
effect of concurrent demand.
If providers do not over-provision sufficiently, practitioners who call APIs during peak hours
may receive degraded latency---increasing costs for time-sensitive applications such as
real-time agents or interactive chat.
Whether throughput degradation also affects output \emph{quality} (answer accuracy) is an
open question with direct implications for reliability-critical deployments.

This paper reports the results of a 28-day empirical study comparing peak-hour and
off-peak-hour performance across four major LLM API providers on a standardized MMLU-Pro
benchmark suite.
Our primary contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A reproducible measurement framework for longitudinal LLM API benchmarking.
    \item Provider-specific quantification of peak-vs-nonpeak latency and throughput effects.
    \item Evidence that MMLU-Pro accuracy is robust to infrastructure load conditions.
    \item An anomalous finding for a non-US-headquartered provider suggesting
          inter-regional load balancing.
\end{enumerate}
We regard this work as a pilot study. Limitations in complete-slot coverage, question-set size,
and network control are acknowledged; a more rigorous follow-up study is planned.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Providers and Models}

We evaluated four providers and their smallest fast-inference models available at the time
of the study (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
We specifically chose lower-cost, high-throughput models under the hypothesis that
budget-tier endpoints---which are more likely to be densely shared---would exhibit greater
sensitivity to peak-hour load than premium offerings.
All models were prompted with a fixed system prompt instructing single-letter responses
(\texttt{A}--\texttt{D}), with \texttt{temperature=0} for reproducibility.
Streaming inference was used for all calls to measure TTFT directly.

\subsection{Question Set}

We drew 30 questions from the \emph{hard} split of MMLU-Pro
\citep{wang2024mmlupro}, an extension of the original MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021measuring}
that increases difficulty and expands each item from four to up to ten answer options.
Our sample is balanced across six subjects (mathematics, physics, engineering, history,
law, and psychology), five questions each; 27 of the 30 items have ten options and three
have nine.
Each question was repeated three times per session to reduce variance, yielding 90 trials
per provider per session (30 questions $\times$ 3 repeats).
Accuracy was scored by extracting the first valid option letter from the model response
using a cascade of regular expressions targeting XML-tagged answers, explicit
``Answer:'' labels, and trailing isolated letters.
We verified that this parser is not a bottleneck: format failures (responses with no
parseable option letter) are rare (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}), and a lenient variant that
additionally maps an answer's option \emph{text} back to a letter recovers essentially no
additional correct answers. Reported accuracy therefore reflects genuine option selection,
not parsing loss.

\subsection{System Prompt}

All providers received the same system prompt, which instructed the model to (1) write
step-by-step reasoning inside a \texttt{<Reason>} tag and (2) output only a single correct
option letter inside an \texttt{<Answer>} tag.
No additional context or few-shot examples were provided.
The prompt was held constant across all sessions and providers to ensure comparability.

\subsection{Experimental Schedule and Session Flow}

Experiments ran from 2026-03-30 to 2026-04-26 (28 days).
Two sessions were scheduled daily in the America/New\_York timezone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peak}: 10:00\,ET --- within the 8\,AM--2\,PM ET peak band \citep{anthropic2026promotion}.
    \item \textbf{Non-peak}: 22:00\,ET --- US East Coast late night.
\end{itemize}
Sessions were managed by a Python scheduler (APScheduler) running as a launchd daemon on
a Mac Mini with a residential internet connection.

Within each session, questions were iterated in a fixed order.
For each question, all four providers were called sequentially in a fixed order
(OpenAI $\to$ Anthropic $\to$ Google $\to$ Qwen), with a 2-second pause between calls
to avoid triggering rate limits.
This cycle was repeated three times per question (\texttt{REPEATS=3}).

\subsection{Resumption Logic}

Each individual API call was recorded as a task in a JSON progress file upon completion.
If a session was interrupted (e.g., due to an API error or system restart), the scheduler
detected the unfinished session on the next run and resumed from the last completed task,
skipping already-recorded calls.
Sessions marked as fully complete were not re-executed.

\subsection{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TTFT} (time-to-first-token, seconds): wall-clock time from request
          dispatch to receipt of the first streamed token.
    \item \textbf{TPS} (tokens per second): output token count divided by generation time
          after the first token.
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: proportion of trials where the parsed answer matches the
          correct MMLU-Pro key.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

The unit of statistical independence is the measurement \emph{slot}, not the individual
API call: the 360 calls within a slot share that session's server-load and network state
and are therefore strongly correlated. Testing at the call level would treat these
correlated observations as independent (pseudoreplication), inflating the effective sample
size by more than two orders of magnitude and yielding spuriously small $p$-values.
We therefore first aggregate each metric to a single value per (slot, provider)---the
median for latency and throughput, the mean for accuracy and format-failure rate---and
then compare peak slots with non-peak slots.
Because only a few slots per condition survive the completeness filter
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), we report two complementary tests: a two-sided exact
Mann--Whitney U test on slot values ($p_\text{exact}$, rank-based) and an exact permutation
test on the difference in slot means ($p_\text{perm}$, magnitude-based and more sensitive
to outlier slots). Effect sizes are reported as slot-level differences and percent change
relative to the non-peak value. We treat $p < 0.05$ as the significance threshold but,
given the slot count, interpret results as suggestive rather than confirmatory.
A linear mixed-effects model with a per-slot random intercept---the principled way to
retain call-level data---could not be reliably fit at the current slot count and is
deferred to a larger follow-up.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Data Completeness}

Of 56 scheduled slots (28 days $\times$ 2 periods), 54 were executed; 3 non-peak slots
were missed due to daemon downtime.
Most executed slots were partial: API errors (primarily Google returning HTTP~429/503)
caused premature termination in 45 of 54 slots.
A \emph{complete} slot is defined as exactly 360 rows
(30 questions $\times$ 3 repeats $\times$ 4 providers).
Only 9 slots were complete (3 non-peak, 6 peak).
All primary results below use these 9 complete slots (3,240 rows total, aggregated to
$9 \times 4 = 36$ slot-level observations);
see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for discussion of this constraint.

All three US-hosted providers show directionally higher TTFT during peak hours
(Table~\ref{tab:ttft}), from $+3.5\%$ (OpenAI) to $+18.1\%$ (Google) in slot medians.
The statistical evidence, however, is weak once the slot is treated as the unit of

consistent shift. OpenAI's increase is not significant under either test.
Qwen is the clear exception: its TTFT is \emph{lower} during peak hours, and this reversal
is significant under both tests---the most robust latency effect in our data---a pattern we
discuss in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

No provider shows a statistically significant change in accuracy between peak and non-peak

Absolute accuracy varies widely across providers, from $\approx$48\% for OpenAI's

The low \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} figure is genuine performance on the hard, ten-option MMLU-Pro

essentially identical to the overall figure ($0.496$ vs.\ $0.484$ for OpenAI), and even an
upper bound that credited \emph{every} unparseable response as correct would raise OpenAI's
accuracy only to $\approx$51\%. The result is consistent with a small, budget-tier model on
a deliberately difficult benchmark.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{US-hosted providers: TTFT trends upward with load.}
OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google all show elevated slot-median TTFT during peak hours,
directionally consistent with increased queuing or scheduling overhead under higher
concurrent demand.
The point estimates range from $\approx$3.5\% for OpenAI to $\approx$18\% for Google,
which could reflect different capacity margins across providers.
We stress, however, that with three to six slots per condition this trend is only weakly
supported: OpenAI's increase is not significant, and Google's---though the largest---does
not survive a magnitude-based test, indicating sensitivity to a small number of
high-latency slots. These effects should be read as hypotheses to be confirmed with the
expanded slot coverage outlined in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
Notably, whatever latency increase exists does not propagate to generation speed or
accuracy, implying that once the model begins generating, execution is largely isolated
from request-level congestion.

\paragraph{Qwen anomaly: possible effect of regional user distribution.}
Qwen's US endpoint (\texttt{dashscope-us.aliyuncs.com}) is operated by Alibaba Cloud,
whose primary user base is concentrated in Asia.
US business peak hours (10:00\,ET) correspond to approximately 23:00--01:00 CST the
following day, well outside Asian business hours.
The fact that Qwen showed lower TTFT and higher TPS precisely during US peak hours is
consistent with the interpretation that US-region capacity is relatively underutilized when
Asian demand is low---though we cannot confirm this from API-level measurements alone.

\paragraph{Accuracy robustness.}
The absence of accuracy degradation during peak hours is practically important.
Applications relying on LLM APIs for classification or reasoning can expect consistent
output quality regardless of the time of day.
Operators optimizing purely for quality need not schedule around traffic patterns.
Those optimizing for latency-sensitive workloads (real-time agents, interactive chat) may
see TTFT increases on the order of 3--18\% during US peak hours for US-hosted providers,
though our data can only establish this trend directionally.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Limited complete-slot coverage and Google data gaps.}
Of 54 executed slots, only 9 met the completeness threshold of 360 rows.
The primary cause was Google Gemini API rate-limiting (HTTP~429/503 errors), which
terminated experiments mid-session, resulting in entire Google response rows being absent
rather than malformed.
This disproportionately affected non-peak slots (only 3 complete) and may introduce
selection bias.
Analyses are restricted to complete slots to minimize this bias, but the sample covers only
a subset of the 28-day window and severely limits statistical power.

\paragraph{Low statistical power and the slot as the unit of analysis.}
Because calls within a slot are correlated, the effective sample size is the number of
complete slots (nine), not the number of calls. With three to six slots per condition, the
smallest attainable two-sided exact $p$-value is $\approx$0.024, so even our
``significant'' effects sit near this floor and cannot be treated as confirmatory.
We report two tests (rank- and magnitude-based) precisely because they can disagree at this
scale, and we interpret all latency and throughput effects as directional pilot signals.
Confirmatory analysis---including the per-slot random-intercept mixed model we could not fit
here---requires substantially more complete slots.


\paragraph{Residential network variability.}
All measurements were made from a Mac Mini on a residential internet connection.
Network jitter contributes to TTFT variance and cannot be fully separated from server-side
latency effects.

\paragraph{Small question set and statistical power.}
Each question was repeated three times per session to reduce per-question noise, but the
pool of 30 MMLU-Pro questions remains small.
This limits coverage across subjects and is insufficient to detect small accuracy effects
($<$2\%) or subject-level differences. A larger and more diverse question set would
improve sensitivity.

\paragraph{Non-comparable generation speed across providers.}
Qwen (\texttt{qwen3.5-flash}) returns extended chain-of-thought reasoning by default,
producing responses substantially longer than those of other providers for the same prompt.
As a result, TPS figures for Qwen reflect a different output regime and direct
cross-provider speed comparisons are not meaningful.
Within-provider peak vs.\ non-peak TPS comparisons remain valid.

\paragraph{Model versioning.}
Providers may silently update model weights or infrastructure during the study period.
Observed trends reflect the combined effect of load patterns and any within-study
infrastructure changes, which we did not control for.

\section{Conclusion}

We conducted a 28-day longitudinal benchmark of four commercial LLM APIs at US business-hour
peak and off-peak times.
Analyzed at the slot level, US-hosted providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) show
directionally elevated TTFT during peak hours (3--18\% in slot medians), with no
corresponding degradation in generation throughput or answer accuracy; this trend is only
marginally significant given nine complete slots.
The most robust finding is that Qwen, served from a US-region Alibaba Cloud endpoint, shows
the opposite pattern---faster TTFT and higher TPS during US peak---significant under both
tests and consistent with cross-regional load balancing.

These findings suggest that practitioners may expect modest latency increases for
latency-critical applications during US business hours but can rely on consistent output
quality regardless of time of day, with the important caveat that the latency effects are
directional pilot signals rather than confirmed results.
This study should be regarded as a pilot investigation. The limited number of complete slots, small question set, and uncontrolled network conditions constrain the generalisability of the results. A follow-up study will address these shortcomings by mitigating API rate limits, controlling for network variability, expanding the question pool, and adopting more rigorous evaluation metrics to place the findings on a firmer empirical footing.
